Question 1: Wildcat records credit score, debt-to-income ratio, and annual income for every borrower, three signals that don't always point the same direction. What should a portfolio committee do when a borrower looks strong on one of these dimensions but weak on another, and which combinations of strength and weakness deserve the most scrutiny versus which can reasonably be treated as offsetting each other?
Summary: Credit score, DTI, and income measure fundamentally different dimensions, historical behavior, current leverage, and total resource scale, meaning divergence among them provides critical diagnostic insight rather than a data conflict. High DTI represents the most dangerous risk signal regardless of a strong credit score or high income, as existing leverage leaves a borrower acutely vulnerable to sudden financial shocks. Conversely, strong income and low leverage can reasonably offset an older, explainable credit blemish, provided there is no active downward trend in credit performance. Furthermore, committees must recognize that DTI risk is non-linear across income levels, as higher earners retain significantly more absolute dollar cushion at elevated debt ratios than lower-income peers. Ultimately, an effective review should prioritize DTI exposure, adjust for absolute income buffers, and establish whether the underwriting data includes historical trends rather than mere point-in-time snapshots.

Summary:Evaluating loan performance solely through an aggregate figure masks critical underlying dynamics, as collateralized, unsecured, and cycle-sensitive loan categories behave fundamentally differently. To prevent blind spots, the committee requires segment-level visibility into volume share, loss distribution, vintage delinquency curves, and underwriting drift across borrower profiles. The necessity of this breakdown is evident in the data, where a seemingly stable overall problem rate between 13% and 18% conceals a sharp, multi-year deterioration in Auto originations climbing from 9% to 31%. This mounting Auto risk is mathematically obscured by the sheer size and relative stability of Home Improvement, which represents 31% of the portfolio. Consequently, the committee faces two distinct parallel challenges: an emerging, masked decline in Auto quality and a persistently high loss concentration in Home Improvement that accounts for 44% of total defaults.

Question 3: Wildcat's status field treats Delinquent and Default as separate states rather than collapsing them into a single "bad loan" bucket, alongside Current and Paid Off. What is the meaningful difference between a delinquent loan and a defaulted one in terms of the borrower relationship and expected recovery, and why does tracking movement between these statuses matter more to a committee than just watching the size of any one bucket at a point in time?
Summary: Delinquency and default represent distinct operational stages and recovery profiles rather than interchangeable labels for bad debt. Delinquent loans reflect an active, potentially curable relationship focused on collections and workouts, whereas defaulted loans represent charged-off or liquidated losses with substantially lower recovery expectations. The underlying data supports this separation, revealing a clear severity gradient where credit scores drop and interest rates climb monotonically from Current through to Default. Tracking movement between these stages via roll rates provides a vital leading indicator, as a static bucket size cannot distinguish between loans curing naturally and loans progressing steadily toward charge-off. However, because this specific dataset is a single cross-sectional snapshot lacking historical transition panels, tracking actual borrower roll rates will require Wildcat to systematically store longitudinal servicing data.

Question 4: Wildcat prices loans across a range of interest rates rather than a single flat rate for each purpose. What should a committee expect to see in the relationship between a loan's rate and the borrower's underlying credit profile, and what would it signal about the portfolio if that relationship broke down, meaning riskier-looking borrowers weren't actually being priced at higher rates?
Summary: A healthy risk-based pricing framework requires higher interest rates to compensate for weaker borrower risk profiles, a principle strongly validated in Wildcat's portfolio by a consistent negative 0.84 correlation between credit scores and interest rates across all product types and vintages. Realized outcomes corroborate this disciplined structure, as average interest rates rise monotonically from Current status up through Default. However, a significant institutional blind spot exists because interest rates show essentially zero correlation with debt-to-income ratios, meaning borrowers with heavy debt burdens receive virtually the same rate as those with ample financial slack. If the core score-to-rate pricing relationship had broken down instead, it would indicate severe structural issues such as uncompensated manual overrides, competitive margin compression, or adverse selection. Ultimately, Wildcat's pricing policy functions soundly at the credit-score level, leaving the unpriced risk of high DTI leverage as the primary target for committee adjustment.

Question 5: Loan term in Wildcat's data doesn't automatically track loan purpose. Some Auto or Personal loans run for many years, just as some Business loans do. What risk does a longer repayment term add on top of what's already captured by credit score, DTI, and income, and should a committee treat term length as a distinct monitoring dimension rather than assuming it's fully explained by purpose and borrower financials?
Summary: Loan duration introduces critical horizon exposure that point-in-time underwriting metrics fail to capture, effectively lengthening the window during which unexpected life shocks can occur while slowing principal amortization. Furthermore, longer terms can artificially suppress monthly payments to mask underlying leverage, creating an optical improvement in DTI ratios that conceals true financial strain. Statistical analysis confirms that loan term operates completely independently within Wildcat’s portfolio, showing near-zero correlation with credit score, DTI, income, loan size, or pricing. Crucially, duration risk disproportionately impacts prime borrowers (scores 700+), whose problem rates climb nearly 30% as loan lengths extend, while remaining uncompensated by higher interest rates. This dynamic represents a recurring institutional blind spot where compounding structural risks, such as extended terms and unpriced leverage, remain hidden beneath seemingly safe, top-tier credit scores.
